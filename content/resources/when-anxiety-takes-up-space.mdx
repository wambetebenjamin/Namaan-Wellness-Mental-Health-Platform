Anxiety has a way of expanding. It begins as a small worry about one thing, and before long it is in the car with you, at your desk, and lying next to you at midnight going through a list.

If that sounds familiar, the first thing worth saying is this: **you are not doing anything wrong.** Anxiety is not a character flaw or a weakness of will. It is your body doing something it was designed to do, at a time when it is not needed.

## What is actually happening

When your brain detects a threat, it activates your sympathetic nervous system. Your heart rate rises, your breathing shallows, blood moves toward your large muscles. This is genuinely useful if the threat is physical and immediate.

The difficulty is that your body cannot tell the difference between a threat that is happening *now* and one you are *imagining for the future*. A difficult conversation on Thursday produces the same physiological response as a car swerving toward you.

This is why "just don't worry about it" never works. You are not being asked to change your thinking. You are being asked to override a physical response.

> The goal is not to never feel anxious. The goal is to feel anxious and still be able to move.

## Three things that help in the moment

**1. Slow the exhale.** You cannot directly slow your heart rate, but you can influence it through your breath. Breathe in for four counts and out for six. The longer exhale signals safety to your nervous system. Do it for ninety seconds.

**2. Name what is happening.** Say to yourself, quietly: *this is anxiety, and it will pass.* This sounds almost too simple, but naming an emotion shifts activity away from the amygdala and toward the parts of your brain that can regulate. You are moving from being inside the feeling to observing it.

**3. Give the worry a time.** Rather than letting anxiety sit across your whole day, tell it: *we will think about this at 5:30.* When the thought arrives at 11am, you acknowledge it and return to what you were doing. Many people find the worry has lost most of its force by the appointed hour.

## When anxiety is worth getting help for

Consider reaching out if any of these are true:

- You are avoiding places, people or tasks you used to manage
- Your sleep has been disturbed for more than a few weeks
- You are having panic attacks
- The anxiety is affecting your relationships or your work
- You are using alcohol, food or overwork to manage it

There is no threshold you have to reach before you deserve support. People come to therapy with mild anxiety that is simply tiresome, and that is a perfectly good reason.

## A note on medication

Medication helps some people considerably and is worth discussing with a psychiatrist or your GP. It is not a failure, and it is not a permanent commitment. Therapy and medication work well together.
