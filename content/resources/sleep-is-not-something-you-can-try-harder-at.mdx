Sleep has a particular cruelty: the more you need it and the harder you try, the less it comes.

This is not a riddle. Effort and sleep are physiologically opposed. Trying to sleep activates the same alerting system that sleep requires you to switch off.

> You cannot decide to fall asleep. You can only create the conditions and let go of the deciding.

## Why you wake at 3am

Waking in the small hours is extremely common, and it is often not a sleep problem at all. It is frequently anxiety, with the night acting as an amplifier — there is nothing to distract you, and everything feels larger at 3am than it will at 9am.

Things that make it worse:

- Looking at the clock and calculating remaining sleep
- Moving to another room in frustration
- Trying to "catch up" by going to bed earlier the next night
- Checking your phone (the light and the content both work against you)

## What actually helps

**Get out of bed if you are awake for long.** This feels wrong, and it is the single most useful change for most people. If you have been awake for what feels like more than twenty minutes, get up, go somewhere dim, and do something undemanding until you feel sleepy. You are protecting the association between bed and sleep.

**Keep a consistent wake time.** Not bedtime — *wake* time, including weekends. It is the anchor your body clock actually uses.

**Get light early.** Morning daylight within an hour of waking is the strongest signal you can give your body clock. Ten minutes outside.

**Write it down before bed.** If your mind races with tasks, spend five minutes writing them down. Not to solve them — to get them out of your head and onto paper where they will wait.

**Lower the temperature.** Most people sleep in rooms that are too warm.

## On screens

The blue-light conversation is often overstated. The more significant problem is what screens do to your *mind* — the stimulation, the comparison, the news, the work email that was not there five minutes ago.

If you use your phone in bed, notice honestly what it is doing to you. Many people find that moving the charger across the room changes more than any app.

## When poor sleep needs more than good habits

Speak to someone if you have had difficulty sleeping most nights for more than a month, if it is affecting your daytime functioning, or if others have noticed you snoring heavily, gasping, or stopping breathing.

Persistent insomnia responds well to CBT-I, a structured talking therapy for sleep that is more effective long-term than sleeping tablets. It is something we can help with.

## One last thing

A bad night is survivable. Most of the distress people feel comes not from the lost sleep but from the catastrophe they build around it during the night — *tomorrow will be ruined, I cannot cope like this.*

You have had bad nights before and you got through the following day. This is not to dismiss how unpleasant it is. It is that the night is lying to you about how bad tomorrow will be.
