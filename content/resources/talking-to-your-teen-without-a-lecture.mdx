"How was school?"

"Fine."

This exchange is so universal that it is almost a genre. It is not that your teenager has nothing to say. It is that the conditions are wrong.

## Why the direct question fails

A face-to-face question with eye contact, from a person who has authority over you, who may act on what you tell them — that is an interview, not a conversation.

Adolescents are also in the middle of a necessary developmental project: becoming separate from you. Answering your questions fully can feel like it works against that.

## Three conditions that work better

**1. Side by side.** In a car, on a walk, washing up together. Not across a table. When you are both looking at the road or the dishes, the pressure of eye contact disappears and words tend to arrive. Most of the important conversations I have heard about happened in a car.

**2. No immediate response required.** Nothing shuts a teenager down faster than the feeling that what they say will instantly become a plan, a consequence, or a worry. Sometimes the whole goal is that they said it out loud.

**3. You go first.** If you want to know about their day, say something true about yours. Not a performance — something real. *Today was a lot and I am tired.* This is not a technique. It is what actually builds the conditions for honesty.

> Connection is not built in the conversations you plan. It is built in the small unremarkable ones you did not.

## Three things to try saying instead

Instead of **"How was school?"** — "What was the most boring part of today?"

Instead of **"Is everything okay?"** — "You have seemed quieter this week. I might be wrong."

Instead of **"You need to sort this out."** — "What do you think would help? I will follow your lead."

## When to be concerned

It is worth seeking support if you notice a sustained change over a few weeks rather than a bad few days:

- Withdrawing from friends and things they used to enjoy
- Sleeping far more or far less than usual
- A marked drop in school engagement
- Changes in eating
- Statements suggesting they do not want to be here
- Increased irritability that does not lift

## What to say if you are worried

Try this shape:

*"I have noticed [specific change]. I am not angry. I am a bit worried, and I wanted to say so. You do not have to explain anything now."*

Then — and this is the hard part — let that be the whole conversation if that is what they choose. You have planted something. Often they come back to it two days later, at an inconvenient moment.

## On their privacy

If your young person is in therapy, the sessions are confidential. We explain the limits to them clearly at the start. Parents are supported separately where that helps, so the young person's space stays genuinely theirs. This is not exclusion — it is what makes the work possible.
